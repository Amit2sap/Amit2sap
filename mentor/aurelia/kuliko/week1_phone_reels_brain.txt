Aurelia's Coach · Week 1 · Your phone, reels and your brain

1. Why reels feel so hard to stop
Short videos give a small "reward" every few seconds: something funny, new or surprising. The brain releases dopamine, a chemical linked to wanting more. Because the next video might be better, it is hard to stop. Apps are designed this way on purpose: endless scroll, autoplay, notifications and streaks keep people on the app longer.

2. What it does to attention
When the brain gets used to a new reward every 15–30 seconds, slower tasks like reading a chapter or practising maths feel boring sooner. Attention is like a muscle: long, focused practice makes it stronger; constant switching makes it weaker. The good news: it recovers when you practise focus again.

3. What it does to sleep
Screens late at night keep the brain alert, and bright light can delay the body's sleep signal (melatonin). Teenagers need about 8–10 hours of sleep. Sleep is when memory is stored, so studying and then sleeping well helps you remember what you learnt.

4. Comparison and mood
Social media mostly shows other people's best moments, edited and filtered. Comparing your real life with their highlights can make you feel worse about yourself. Notice how you feel after 30 minutes of scrolling: better, the same, or worse?

5. Phone as a tool, not a boss
- Keep the phone in another room while studying.
- Phone on the charger outside your bedroom at night.
- Turn off non-essential notifications.
- Use the phone's own screen-time timer and set your own limit.
- Swap one scroll session a day for something you love: 20 minutes of guitar, drawing or a swim.

6. Your experiment this week
Write down your screen-time number today. Try one phone-free study block a day and one phone-free night. At the end of the week, check: did you sleep better, finish work faster, feel calmer?
